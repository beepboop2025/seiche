Money-market rates: compare benchmarks and calculate funding cost

By Seiche · Published and source-checked 4 October 2026 · Educational research

Compare money-market rates only after identifying their currency, tenor, collateral, participants and observation date. A benchmark describes its underlying market; it is not a borrowing offer for every institution. To translate an annualised rate into a cost, state the principal, term, day-count convention, compounding method and included fees.

Why are SOFR, SONIA and euro short-term rates different?

SOFR describes overnight Treasury-secured dollar borrowing. The Bank of England's SONIA measures sterling overnight borrowing based on eligible transactions. The ECB publishes the euro short-term rate, €STR, with its own transaction, settlement and publication conventions.

These rates are not interchangeable merely because each is overnight and annualised. Currency, collateral and the eligible participants differ. A numerical gap between countries is not an immediately available arbitrage profit; funding access, hedging, balance-sheet costs and settlement matter.

Worked example: the lowest rate need not give the lowest included cost

Hypothetical, same-currency financing scenarios. For principal of 1,000,000 units, a 6% annual simple rate over 30 actual days on a 360-day basis costs 5,000 units before fees. A 5% rate costs about 4,166.67 units; adding 1,000 units of fees raises that scenario's included cost to about 5,166.67.

The simple-interest formula is principal × annual_rate_pct / 100 × actual_days / basis. With a 365-day basis, the first scenario instead costs about 4,931.51. That difference comes from the convention, not a change in the headline rate.

The New York Fed's reference-rate methodology explains the separate treatment of daily compounding and non-business days in its published SOFR averages. A constant-rate simple-interest scenario does not reproduce those averages or the terms of an individual contract.
